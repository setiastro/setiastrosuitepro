Finder Chart

Generates a labelled finder chart for the current field by fetching a cutout of a published sky survey (DSS2, Pan-STARRS, SDSS, DESI Legacy, 2MASS, Gaia DR3, or SIMG's Northern-Sky Narrowband Survey) through the CDS hips2fits service, overlaying the user's own doc with a WCS-correct perspective warp, and annotating with bright-star names, deep-sky catalogue objects, an N/E compass, a scale bar, and optionally the imaging train's field-of-view rectangle. Two entry points: driven from an open SASpro doc (uses the doc's WCS to compute the chart's centre and FOV), or driven directly from RA/Dec (used by object-search workflows where no image exists yet).

Workflow
Open the Finder Chart dialog on a plate-solved SASpro view, or launch the coord variant from an object-search dialog. Pick a survey — DSS2 color is a safe default; swap to narrowband NSNS if the field is nebular. Pick a scale — 2× / 4× / 8× the current doc's FOV. Toggle overlays — bright stars, deep-sky objects, compass, scale bar, grid, imaging-train FOV box. Save PNG or Send to New Document to open the finished chart as a brand-new SASpro view.

The signature move is the WCS-correct perspective warp of your own data onto the published survey:

(x_{\mathrm{doc}}, y_{\mathrm{doc}}) \overset{\mathrm{WCS}_{\mathrm{doc}}}{\to} (\alpha, \delta) \overset{\mathrm{WCS}_{\mathrm{bg}}}{\to} (x_{\mathrm{bg}}, y_{\mathrm{bg}})

Your doc's four corners are projected through its own WCS to sky coordinates, then those sky coordinates are projected through the background's WCS back to pixels in the finder chart. The four corner correspondences give the homography, OpenCV warps the whole doc into the background frame, and an alpha-blend composite shows your image at any requested opacity. The result: the published survey tile and your own data occupy the same arcseconds of sky at the same pixel resolution, so your target lands exactly where you shot it — useful for publication figures (“here's my image, here's where it sits in the DSS”), for cross-checking a plate solve against a known survey, or for annotating your field with catalogue objects you can navigate.

1. What it's for

Finder charts have three main uses in astrophotography:

  • Publication figures — showing your image in context next to or on top of a well-known survey (DSS2) so viewers can locate the target in the sky.
  • Target annotation — labelling the catalogue objects visible in your field, so the viewer knows what each thing is.
  • Pre-session planning / cross-checking — verifying that a plate-solved field actually corresponds to what the catalogue says is there, or planning a composition from a known RA/Dec before any data has been captured.

SASpro's Finder Chart handles all three. It pulls a cutout of a published sky survey centred on your field, overlays your own image onto it with the correct WCS alignment, and annotates with bright-star names, deep-sky objects, compass direction, pixel scale, and (optionally) the FOV rectangle of your imaging train.

2. Two entry points

From an open doc (FinderChartDialog)

Opens with the current SASpro view's WCS as the reference. The chart's centre is the view's centre pixel projected to sky; the chart's FOV is derived from the view's four corners. Scale is a multiple of the doc's own FOV: 2×, 4×, or 8×. The doc image is overlaid onto the background at user-configurable opacity.

From RA/Dec (FinderChartFromCoordDialog)

Driven by a (name, RA, Dec) tuple instead of a doc. Used by SASpro's object-search workflows (WIMS) where the user is browsing a catalogue and wants to see what a candidate target looks like before loading any data. No doc overlay — just the survey background and overlays. FOV is picked from a dropdown of standard values (0.25° to 10°).

4. Size / FOV

Doc-driven dialog: Size dropdown with three multipliers of the current doc's FOV — 2× (zoom in, context around the frame), 4× (default, generous surround), 8× (wide context).

Coord dialog: FOV dropdown with ten absolute values from 0.25° to 10°. Default 4°.

Changes trigger a refetch because the request to the HiPS service has to be redone at the new angular size.

5. Output resolution

Output px spinbox (300 to 2400, step 100, default 900): the pixel size of the finished chart. The HiPS service is asked for this many pixels on a side; your doc gets resampled through the perspective warp to match. Higher is sharper but slower to fetch and heavier to save. For saving at publication quality, 1800–2400 px is typical.

6. Image opacity

(Doc-driven dialog only.) Slider from 0% to 100%, default 35%. Controls how much of your own data is blended onto the background:

  • 0% — pure survey background. Useful when you want a plain reference finder chart with your field outlined.
  • 35% — your data gently layered onto the survey. Useful for “this is my shot, here's its context” style figures.
  • 100% — your data at full strength over the survey. The survey still shows outside your frame as context.

Slider movements debounce the render (no refetch) and are additionally gated on sliderReleased for the final high-quality render.

7. Bright-star labels

Check Star names to label every bright star in the chart's area. Two parameters:

  • Star ≤ magnitude limit (-2.0 to 8.0, default 5.0). Dimmer stars are not labelled.
  • Max label count (5 to 200, default 30). After radius and magnitude filtering, the brightest-first list is capped at this count.

The source is SASpro's packaged bright-star table (same used by other SASpro tools). Labels are placed with a gold dot marker and the star's common name (Vega, Altair, Betelgeuse, …). Decluttering keeps nearby bright stars from stacking their labels on top of each other.

8. Deep-sky overlay

Check Deep-sky to annotate the catalogued objects in the field. Three parameters:

  • Catalogue dropdown: “All (DSO)”, Messier, NGC, IC, Abell, SH2 (Sharpless 2), LBN (Lynds Bright Nebulae), LDN (Lynds Dark Nebulae), PN-G (Planetary Nebula Galactic).
  • Mag ≤ magnitude limit (-2.0 to 30.0, default 12.0).
  • Max label count (5 to 300, default 30).

DSOs get a cyan square marker, a label, and — where the catalogue entry includes a size — a cyan outline circle at the object's major-axis diameter in sky arcminutes converted through the chart's pixel scale. The circle gives a visual sense of how big the object is in your field.

9. Compass and scale bar

Check Compass to draw N/E direction arrows in the bottom-right corner. The arrows are built from the actual background WCS by stepping a small sky offset and projecting it to pixels, so they correctly point to true north and east on the chart including any rotation or near-polar distortion.

Check Scale bar to draw a labelled scale bar in the bottom-left corner. The bar's physical length is chosen from the FOV (30′ / 20′ / 10′ / 5′), and the label shows that length plus the pixel scale as arcsec/px.

10. Grid

Check Show grid to overlay an RA/Dec grid using Astropy's WCSAxes. The grid uses the background's WCS so it draws correctly curved near the poles and projects straight along the equator; tick labels are RA and Dec in standard decimal format.

11. Imaging-train FOV box

Check Show FOV box to draw a labelled rectangle showing where a specific imaging train would frame the field. Five parameters:

  • Focal length (mm) — your scope's focal length.
  • Pixel pitch (µm) — your camera's pixel pitch.
  • Sensor (px W×H) — your sensor's dimensions in pixels.
  • Rotation (°CW) — the camera rotation measured clockwise from north. 0° = sensor's long axis along east-west.

As you adjust any parameter, a live FOV readout appears next to the controls showing the resulting width × height in arcminutes. The box itself updates on the chart on debounce (no network required). Useful for pre-session planning: dial in different scope/camera combinations and see which gives you the composition you want.

12. Save and send

Save PNG…

Writes the current chart as a PNG at the full output resolution. The last-used directory is remembered across launches.

Send to New Document (doc-driven dialog only)

Opens the chart as a brand-new SASpro view, complete with metadata recording how it was generated (survey, scale multiplier, grid state, output resolution, overlay opacity). Useful when you want to further process the chart (annotate manually, crop, export at a different size).

13. Technical details

WCS recovery, HiPS fetch and cache, overlay warp, and the overlay-rendering pipeline in order.

13.1 WCS recovery

SASpro's metadata model stores WCS information in several possible forms depending on how a doc was created, so the Finder Chart walks a four-tier fallback chain:

  1. A prebuilt astropy.wcs.WCS object under meta["wcs"] (fastest path — the Plate Solver stores one here after a successful solve).
  2. A nested astropy.io.fits.Header under meta["original_header"], meta["fits_header"], meta["header"], meta["FITS Header"], or meta["FITS_HEADER"].
  3. Flattened keys like "FITS Header.CTYPE1" = "RA---TAN-SIP" coming from CSV-exported metadata — SASpro rebuilds a Header from these by stripping the prefix.
  4. Return None — the finder chart renders with no WCS, which falls back to a plain pixel grid and no catalogue overlays.

When a header is reconstructed, SASpro strips a set of non-FITS metadata keys that are sometimes stored alongside real cards (FILE_PATH, FITS_HEADER, BIT_DEPTH, WCS_HEADER, etc.) so the resulting WCS() construction doesn't blow up on invalid keywords. The celestial subspace of the WCS is used when the full WCS has extra axes (spectral, polarisation).

13.2 Footprint and FOV estimation

Given a WCS and image dimensions, the four corner pixels (0.5, 0.5), (W-0.5, 0.5), (W-0.5, H-0.5), (0.5, H-0.5) are projected to sky through pixel_to_world, giving a SkyCoord array of corners and the centre pixel. Angular separations across opposite edges give the width and height:

\cos\sigma = \sin\delta_1 \sin\delta_2 + \cos\delta_1 \cos\delta_2 \cos(\alpha_1 - \alpha_2)

Width is the average of top-edge and bottom-edge separations; height is the average of left-edge and right-edge separations. The final FOV used for the fetch is max(width, height) × scale_mult, so a highly rectangular doc still gets a square cutout that covers its longer dimension at the chosen scale.

13.3 HiPS fetch

SASpro fetches survey cutouts from CDS's hips2fits HTTP service, which renders HiPS-format sky tiles into a flat FITS cutout at the requested projection, FOV, and resolution. The request URL encodes the HiPS catalogue identifier, the output dimensions, the FOV, the TAN projection, ICRS coordinate system, the centre RA/Dec, and format=fits. Response is a FITS file in bytes; SASpro opens it from memory with astropy.io.fits.open(BytesIO(data)), extracts the first HDU's data and header, and builds a WCS from the header.

Data normalisation: NaN/inf scrubbed, converted to RGB (grey → grey×3, channel-first → channel-last if needed), percentile-stretched to 1st/99.5th percentile and clipped to [0, 1] float32. The stretch is appropriate for display; the normalised data is held as float01 for every subsequent cache and overlay operation.

13.4 NSNS narrowband path

The Northern-Sky Narrowband Survey (NSNS) tiles live at simg.de/nebulae3/..., not at CDS. CDS's hips2fits service accepts an arbitrary HiPS base URL as the hips parameter, so SASpro routes NSNS fetches by handing hips2fits the simg.de URL directly. The internal survey ID uses an SIMG/ prefix (SIMG/simg.de/P/NSNS/DR0_1/tc8, etc.) that the dispatcher detects, strips, and maps to the corresponding http://www.simg.de/nebulae3/dr0_1/tc8 URL for the hips2fits call. From hips2fits' perspective it's a normal remote HiPS source; from the user's perspective they just picked “NSNS DR0.2 (Hα)” from the survey dropdown and SASpro figured out the plumbing.

13.5 Overfetch and square crop

A square cutout at exactly the requested FOV would leave the chart's corners unmapped once overlays apply any rotation or when the doc's footprint is rotated against the HiPS tile grid. SASpro overfetches at a factor of √2 plus a 2% safety margin:

s_{\mathrm{over}} = \sqrt{2} \cdot 1.02 \approx 1.44

and scales both the FOV and the pixel count by this amount:

\mathrm{FOV}_{\mathrm{fetch}} = s_{\mathrm{over}} \cdot \mathrm{FOV}_{\mathrm{out}},\;\; N_{\mathrm{fetch}} = \lceil s_{\mathrm{over}} \cdot N_{\mathrm{out}} \rceil

which preserves the pixel scale (arcsec/pixel) across the overfetch. Then centre-crops back to the requested output size, with the WCS adjusted by the crop origin:

\mathrm{CRPIX}' = \mathrm{CRPIX} - (x_0,\; y_0)

— a plain pixel offset, since the CRPIX axis convention matches between FITS and the Python array slice. The crop happens on the cached full-resolution raster, so overlays that redraw don't trigger a refetch.

13.6 Three-tier cache

Three independent caches, each keyed on exactly the parameters that invalidate it:

CacheHoldsKeyInvalidated by
HiPS rasterThe fetched (and crop-corrected) background image + WCS + error stringsurvey, output px, centre RA/Dec, fetch FOVSurvey change, resolution change, FOV change, re-centre
Base rasterThe composed uint8 RGB array that goes into matplotlib's imshow — either plain background or background + warped doc overlayHiPS cache key + opacity + doc identityAny of the above, plus overlay opacity change or doc swap
Doc geometryDoc's WCS, four sky corners, centre, FOVImage shape, WCS object id, size multiplierDoc change, size multiplier change

Why three tiers: a user adjusting the deep-sky magnitude slider should not retrigger an HTTP fetch and a perspective warp of their image — both expensive. The matplotlib layer (markers, labels, compass, scale bar, grid) is cheap. Separating those costs lets interactive adjustments stay snappy. Changing a different parameter down the dependency chain invalidates everything above it; the three-tier structure means the right invariant level is kept whatever the change.

13.7 Debounced render pipeline

Each UI control is routed to one of two paths:

  • Fetch-trigger — survey, size/FOV, output pixels. Immediate render with force_refetch=True, which invalidates the HiPS and base-raster caches.
  • Render-trigger — grid, star names, deep-sky, compass, scale bar, FOV box, opacity. Scheduled on a 150 ms single-shot QTimer, no refetch. Multiple events inside the debounce window coalesce into a single re-render.

Opacity is a special case: the slider sends many valueChanged events during a drag, so SASpro additionally hooks sliderReleased for the final clean render once the user settles. Status label updates immediately on any debounced trigger so the user sees “Rendering…” feedback even before the timer fires.

13.8 Doc overlay perspective warp

When the user wants their data visible on the chart, SASpro overlays it by projecting the doc through its own WCS to sky coordinates, then through the background WCS back to pixels. The four corner pixels of the doc are the source points; their projection to the background's pixel grid gives the destination points. OpenCV's getPerspectiveTransform builds a 3×3 homography:

\mathbf{H} \cdot \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{src}} = \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{dst}}

that maps every doc pixel to a background pixel, and warpPerspective renders the warped doc into the background's shape. Alpha blend using a content-presence mask:

I_{\mathrm{out}} = (1 - \alpha \cdot M) \cdot I_{\mathrm{bg}} + \alpha \cdot M \cdot I_{\mathrm{doc,warped}}

where M is 1 where the warped doc has content and 0 elsewhere (so no artefact in the uncovered regions, and the alpha gives the opacity in the covered ones).

Why a homography and not a simple affine: the two WCS projections introduce a nonlinear plate-scale variation across the doc's footprint if either is far from the tangent point. The homography captures the first-order perspective distortion, which is what WCS projections look like locally over a few-degree FOV.

13.9 Deep-sky catalogue loader

SASpro ships a single celestial_catalog.csv that merges Messier, NGC, IC, Abell, Sharpless 2, LBN, LDN, and PN-G objects into one table with a Catalog column identifying each row's source. The loader opens the file with a decoding ladder — latin-1 → utf-8-sig → utf-8 → cp1252 — so it works whether the file was saved from Excel on Windows, Numbers on Mac, or exported by a Python script. BOM-tolerant column lookup walks "Name", "NAME", and the UTF-8 BOM-prefixed "\ufeffName" for every expected field, so a BOM at the start of the first column doesn't lose that column.

Parsed fields per row: name, RA/Dec (degrees), magnitude (optional), Info / Diameter (optional, often contains size like “6.0x4.0” arcmin), Type / LongType (optional), Catalog (source), and a canonical catalogue code (see next section).

13.10 Catalogue code canonicalisation

Users (and catalogue sources) spell catalogue codes inconsistently:

CatalogueVariants seen in the wildCanonical
Sharpless 2SH2-, SH2 , SH-2, SH 2, SH_2, SHARPLESSSH2
Planetary Nebula GalacticPNG, PN-G, PN G, PN_GPN-G

SASpro's _canon_catalog_code looks at both the Catalog column and the object's Name prefix and reduces every variant to the canonical form. The user's UI filter (dropdown with the clean canonical names) matches against this canonical code, so a mixed-source catalogue CSV filters correctly without hand-cleaning the data. Other catalogues (NGC, IC, M, Abell, LBN, LDN) are already consistent in their standard spelling and pass through unchanged.

13.11 Angular + magnitude filters

For every DSO (and bright star) in the catalogue:

  1. Fast cull: skip if the declination differs from the chart centre by more than radius + 2° — a cheap pre-filter that avoids computing full angular separations for most objects.
  2. Angular separation: compute σ(obj, centre) and keep if:
\sigma(\mathrm{obj},\; \mathrm{centre}) \leq 0.75 \cdot \mathrm{FOV}

The 0.75 factor restricts labels to the inner three-quarters of the chart radius, since labels near the exact edge would clip against the chart boundary.

3. Magnitude cut:

m \leq m_{\max} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \mathrm{keep}

Objects with no magnitude in the catalogue get a sentinel score of 99 so they sort to the end and are kept only if the user's label cap allows.

4. Sort by brightness and keep the top 4 × max_labels — a bit extra over the user's cap, so the declutter step has candidates to pick from in dense regions.

13.12 Declutter algorithm

After brightness-sort, each candidate's chart-pixel position is reduced to a grid cell:

\mathrm{cell}(x, y) = \left( \lfloor x / c \rfloor,\; \lfloor y / c \rfloor \right),\;\; \mathrm{keep\ first\ per\ cell}

with c = 34 px for DSOs, c = 28 px for stars. First object in each cell wins (which is the brightest, since the list was pre-sorted). This stops two bright nearby objects from stacking their labels on top of each other — the brighter one keeps the real estate, the dimmer one gets dropped. Simple, deterministic, no optimisation loop.

The grid-cell approach is deliberately coarser than labels can get. In an open star cluster with a dozen 4th-magnitude stars inside a 100-pixel region, labels would otherwise form an unreadable pile; the grid gives one label per 28-px cell maximum, which comes out at a readable spacing on a 900–2400 px chart.

13.13 In-bounds label placer

Even post-declutter, a label near the chart boundary can overflow because labels are drawn at a pixel offset from the marker and the text itself has width. SASpro's _place_label_inside:

  1. Places the label at the default offset (6 px right, 4 px up from the marker).
  2. Measures its bounding box in display coordinates via get_window_extent(renderer).
  3. Computes the shift needed on each axis to pull the box inside the chart with a 4-pixel padding margin.
  4. Applies the shift.
  5. Re-measures to confirm the result is inside.
  6. If step 5 still shows the label outside the chart (long label that can't fit after a shift), removes the label entirely rather than letting it clip at the edge.

The renderer is obtained from a fig.canvas.draw() call that happens once per chart render, before any label is drawn. Running this check per-label on hundreds of labels is fast because the renderer is already warm.

13.14 DSO size circles

When the catalogue entry has an Info field with a size string like "6.0x4.0" or "90x40", SASpro parses it as arcminute dimensions with a regex, takes the major axis as the honest diameter (since no PA information is available), converts to pixels at the chart's scale:

d_{\mathrm{px}} = \frac{d_{\mathrm{arcmin}} \cdot 60}{s}

and draws a cyan outline circle at that radius centred on the object. The 60-factor converts arcminutes to arcseconds so it divides by s in arcsec/pixel. Circles under 4 px diameter are not drawn (would just look like a marker). The outline colour is a dark cyan (#5145ff) that's visible on bright and dark backgrounds alike.

13.15 Bright-star overlay

The bright-star table is SASpro's shared BRIGHT_STARS list (~200 named stars with proper names, RA, Dec, V-magnitude). Same filter pipeline as DSOs: declination fast-cull, angular separation gate at 0.75×FOV, magnitude cut, sort by V-magnitude, cap at max_labels, declutter at 28-px cells, in-bounds label placement. Marker is a small amber dot (#ffb000) with the star name in the same amber.

Default magnitude limit 5.0: catches the eye-visible stars for naked-eye-scale charts and all the named bright stars (Vega, Deneb, Altair, Betelgeuse…). For wider-field charts with less busy backgrounds, raising to 6.0 or 7.0 catches more secondary stars.

13.16 Compass construction

The N/E arrows are built from the background WCS by stepping a small sky offset from the chart centre in two directions and projecting both to pixels:

  1. Pick a step size: step = max(0.01, min(0.15, FOV × 0.08)) degrees. Scales with the chart's FOV so the compass arrows always have a visible length regardless of zoom.
  2. North endpoint: chart centre + step in Dec.
  3. East endpoint: chart centre + step / cos(dec) in RA. The cos(dec) compensation makes it a real arc-length step on the sky at the current declination (so East looks east regardless of latitude).
  4. Project the centre and both endpoints through the background WCS to chart pixels.
  5. Normalise each vector to a fixed length (9% of chart width) and draw as arrow annotations.
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_N = \frac{\mathbf{v}_N}{\|\mathbf{v}_N\|},\;\; \hat{\mathbf{v}}_E = \frac{\mathbf{v}_E}{\|\mathbf{v}_E\|}

Why not just “up and right”: the chart's pixel axes don't always align with sky North and East — a HiPS cutout can land rotated, and your doc's axes don't match the HiPS cutout either. Deriving the compass from the actual WCS projection guarantees correctness at any orientation.

13.17 Scale bar

Four-tier bar length based on fetch FOV:

FOV ≥Bar length
4° (240′)30′
2° (120′)20′
1° (60′)10′
< 1°5′

Bar length in pixels is bar_arcmin × 60 / s where s is the pixel scale derived from proj_plane_pixel_scales (median of the two axis scales, in degrees per pixel, converted to arcsec):

s = \sqrt{\left| \det \mathrm{CD} \right|} \cdot 3600 \;\; [\mathrm{arcsec/pix}]

Drawn as a white horizontal line with end ticks and a label showing the bar's length in arcminutes plus the pixel scale in arcsec/pixel. Example: "30′ (1.69′′/px)" on a 4° DSS2 chart.

13.18 FOV box math

The imaging-train FOV rectangle is specified by focal length f (mm), pixel pitch p (µm), sensor dimensions N_W × N_H (pixels), and rotation φ (degrees clockwise from north). Angular half-sizes per axis:

\frac{\theta_W}{2} = \arctan\!\left( \frac{p \cdot N_W}{2 \cdot f} \right) \;\; [\mathrm{deg}]

(same for height; the pitch × N gives sensor dimension in micrometres which divided by 2f is the half-angle tangent). The four corners in East-North offset space are (±θ_W/2, ±θ_H/2), then rotated clockwise about the field centre by φ:

\mathbf{d}'_r = (d_E \cos\phi - d_N \sin\phi,\;\; d_E \sin\phi + d_N \cos\phi)

and converted to RA/Dec by compensating RA for the local cosine of declination (so East offset is a real arc-length step):

\alpha_c = \alpha_0 - \frac{d_E'}{\cos\delta_0},\quad \delta_c = \delta_0 + d_N'

The minus sign on d_E is because +East is -RA in standard conventions. Each corner's RA/Dec is then projected through the background WCS to chart pixels, the four pixel points are connected and closed as a polygon, and the diagonal centre gets a text label showing width′ × height′ arcminutes.

The cos(δ) compensation matters near the celestial poles. Without it, a 30′ East offset at Dec 80° would be plotted as a 30′ shift in RA, which is actually only ~5.2′ of arc at that declination; the FOV box would appear squashed. With compensation the box lands correctly at any declination.
